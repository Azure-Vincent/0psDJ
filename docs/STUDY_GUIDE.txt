CYBER OWL LAB - STUDY GUIDE
How to study infrastructure so it sticks
==========================================================================

This guide explains study habits that learning research has shown to work,
and how to apply each one in Cyber Owl Lab. Use it alongside the roadmap on
the "Start here" page: the roadmap tells you WHAT to study next, this guide
tells you HOW to study it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE SHORT VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * Test yourself instead of re-reading.
  * Study a little, often, and come back to old topics later.
  * Mix subjects once you know the basics of each.
  * Ask "why does this work?" and answer in your own words.
  * Practise on machines, not only on paper.
  * Troubleshoot from evidence: look first, then decide, then change.
  * Track what you get wrong and go back to it.
  * Sleep, take breaks, and remove distractions while you study.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. PROVEN STUDY HABITS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 Retrieval practice (test yourself)
    Pulling an answer out of memory strengthens it far more than reading
    the same page again. Re-reading feels productive because the text looks
    familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to use it.

    In the lab:
      - Answer the "Check yourself" questions in your head BEFORE you click
        "Show answer".
      - Take the Assessment tab of each lesson without looking back at the
        lesson first. Then read the sections it links to for anything missed.
      - Before typing a command, say what output you expect. Compare.
      - Close the lesson and explain the topic on paper: "How does a client
        get an address from DHCP across a router?"

2.2 Spaced practice (spread it out)
    Several short sessions spread over days beat one long session. Forgetting
    a little between sessions and then recalling it is what makes memory
    durable.

    In the lab:
      - Aim for regular sessions (for example 30-60 minutes, most days)
        rather than one long weekend session.
      - When you start a new lesson, first spend five minutes on one ticket
        or assessment question from an earlier lesson.
      - Revisit a finished lesson's assessment after about a day, a week and
        a month. The dashboard's "Understanding by concept" table shows which
        concepts are weakest: start there.

2.3 Interleaving (mix topics)
    Once you know the basics of several subjects, practising them mixed
    together teaches you to recognise WHICH tool or idea a problem needs,
    which is the real skill in operations work.

    In the lab:
      - After the first phases of the roadmap, pick troubleshooting tickets
        from different lessons in one session instead of all from one.
      - The integrated labs are interleaving by design: DNS, routing,
        firewalls and services fail together, as they do in real life.

2.4 Elaboration (ask why and how)
    Connect new facts to what you already know. Ask "why does this happen?",
    "what would break if this were missing?", "how is this like something I
    already understand?"

    In the lab:
      - Use the Dependencies stage of the integrated labs: for each hop on a
        request path, say what it needs from the hop before it.
      - Read "How it works" before "Deploy": knowing the mechanism makes the
        configuration steps make sense instead of being a list to memorise.
      - Write in the Notes box why a fix worked, not only what you typed.

2.5 Concrete examples and dual coding (words plus pictures)
    Abstract ideas are easier to learn from specific examples, and combining
    words with diagrams gives you two ways to recall the same idea.

    In the lab:
      - Use the Visualize tab and the Packet Lab to see what a description
        means on the wire.
      - Draw the network from the topology view yourself, from memory, and
        compare. Add addresses, gateways and which service runs where.
      - Build small networks in the Infrastructure Builder and trace packets.

2.6 Deliberate practice (work at the edge of your ability)
    Improvement comes from practice that is slightly too hard, with clear
    goals and fast feedback - not from repeating what you can already do.

    In the lab:
      - Repeat integrated labs in a harder mode: guided, then assisted, then
        independent.
      - Use hints one at a time, only after you are stuck, and try again
        after each one. Avoid "Do it for me" unless you are truly blocked;
        then redo that step yourself later.
      - When a check fails, read its detail and change one thing at a time.

2.7 Metacognition (know what you know)
    Learners often overestimate what they understand. Checking yourself
    honestly tells you where to spend time.

    In the lab:
      - Before checking a step or submitting a diagnosis, rate your
        confidence (sure / unsure / guessing). Compare with the result.
      - Keep an error log (see section 5). Patterns in your mistakes are the
        best guide to what to review.
      - Review concepts marked "struggling" or "learning" on the dashboard
        before starting new material.

2.8 Learn by teaching
    Explaining a topic to someone else (or to an imaginary beginner) exposes
    gaps quickly.

    In the lab:
      - After each phase of the roadmap, explain its goal in a few sentences
        as if to a new colleague, without notes.
      - Study with a partner: one breaks something in a lab, the other
        diagnoses it.

2.9 Look after the basics: sleep, breaks, focus
    Memory is consolidated during sleep, attention fades in long unbroken
    sessions, and switching between tasks costs more than it seems.

      - Study in focused blocks (for example 25-50 minutes) with short breaks.
      - Silence notifications and close unrelated tabs while studying.
      - Do not trade sleep for extra study before an exam or deadline.
      - Physical activity and regular meals help concentration.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. A TROUBLESHOOTING HABIT (the one that matters most in this field)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The lab rewards the way experienced engineers work. Practise it every time:

  1. Reproduce: confirm the symptom yourself. What exactly fails, for whom?
  2. Scope: what still works? Compare a working path with the broken one.
  3. Gather evidence: status, logs, connectivity tests, configuration.
     (ip, ss, ping, dig, curl, journalctl, systemctl status, nft list ...)
  4. Locate: find the first layer that does not work - everything above it
     will fail too. The request-path probes show where traffic stops.
  5. Hypothesise: name a cause that explains ALL the evidence.
  6. Change one thing, then verify from the user's side.
  7. Make it persistent and prove it survives a reboot.
  8. Write down the root cause and how you would prevent it.

Avoid: restarting services "to see if it helps", changing several things at
once, and declaring success because a command printed no error. The lab
tracks restarts before a diagnosis and comments on them for this reason.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. A WEEKLY ROUTINE (example - adapt it to your time)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Each session (30-60 min):
    5 min   Warm-up: one question or ticket from an EARLIER lesson.
    20-40   New material: the next roadmap step (lesson sections, terminal
            practice, or a lab step).
    5-10    Self-test: answer the "Check yourself" questions or a few
            assessment questions without looking back.
    2 min   Write one line in your log: what you learned, what is unclear.

  Once a week:
    - One troubleshooting ticket or integrated-lab incident without hints.
    - Review the dashboard's weakest concepts and redo their assessments.
    - Redo one step you completed with "Do it for me", on your own.

  End of each roadmap phase:
    - Explain the phase goal aloud without notes.
    - Do the phase's integrated lab (or redo it in a harder mode).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. KEEPING AN ERROR LOG
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

For every wrong answer, failed check or wrong diagnosis, write:

  Date | Topic | What I thought | What was true | Why I was wrong | Fix

Re-read the log weekly. Recurring entries (for example "forgot the firewall
on the return path", "changed the file but not the running state") show the
habits to correct. Retest those topics first.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. HOW TO USE EACH PART OF THE LAB
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Start here       Follow the roadmap in order; it never assumes something
                   you have not learned yet.
  Lessons          Read the sections in order. Type the commands yourself in
                   the terminals rather than only reading them.
  Assessments      Attempt first, then read the linked sections for misses.
  Tickets          Diagnose before fixing. Use hints sparingly.
  Guided labs      Build step by step; read why each check passes or fails.
  Integrated labs  Probe the request paths, then troubleshoot the incident.
                   Repeat in assisted and independent mode.
  Case studies     Read the symptoms, predict the cause, then read on.
  Dashboard        Use it to choose what to review, not as a score to chase.
  Notes            Record evidence and reasoning; they are saved with your
                   progress.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. COMMON TRAPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * Re-reading and highlighting instead of testing yourself.
  * Cramming everything into one long session.
  * Copying commands without predicting what they will do.
  * Skipping the "How it works" sections to get to configuration faster.
  * Using "Do it for me" or the reference solution as the first resort.
  * Counting a lesson as learned because every section was opened.
  * Studying only the topics you already like.
